# Why Random Sampling Matters in Data Analysis: A Polymarket Case Study

We drew five random samples from the same Polymarket dataset at three different sizes: 100 markets, 1,000 markets, and 10,000 markets.

The results did not settle into a neat progression.

One sample of 100 markets estimated average volume at $175,256.77. Another sample of exactly the same size estimated it at only $17,819.53—nearly ten times lower. Both samples were selected correctly. Both came from the same population. And neither result was a software error.

The disagreement revealed something more useful: **a random sample can be valid without looking representative, especially when a small number of extreme observations dominate the population.**

Random sampling is a probability-based method for selecting a subset of observations from a larger dataset. When it is designed correctly, it gives every eligible observation an equal chance of selection. That makes large datasets easier to explore and helps prevent researchers from choosing only the rows that support the conclusion they expect.

But randomness does not make uncertainty disappear. Sample size, population shape, rare observations, and the statistic being estimated all affect what a particular sample will show.

To demonstrate this with real data, we used Lychee to repeatedly sample 383,707 closed markets from the [Polymarket Historical archive](/polymarket-historical-data). The experiment produced a practical lesson in random sampling, representative samples, sample size, the mean, the median, and the statistical power of a few enormous prediction markets.

## The short version

- Simple random sampling gave every eligible Polymarket market the same chance of being included.
- Larger samples generally produced more stable estimates, but an individual larger sample was not guaranteed to be closer to the population value.
- Polymarket market volume was highly right-skewed: 1.38% of markets generated 70.15% of total volume.
- The sample median stabilized faster than the sample mean because a few exceptionally large markets strongly affected the mean.
- A random sample describes **how observations were selected**. A representative sample describes **how well the resulting sample reflects relevant characteristics of the population**.

## What is random sampling?

**Random sampling is a selection method in which observations are chosen from a defined population using a random process.** In a simple random sample, every eligible observation has the same probability of selection, and every possible sample of a fixed size has the same probability of being drawn.

That final condition matters. “We grabbed some rows that looked random” is not the same as a simple random sample.

Suppose a dataset contains `N` eligible rows and we select `n` of them using simple random sampling without replacement. The inclusion probability for any individual row is:

`P(row is included) = n / N`

For our production experiment:

- The population contained 383,707 eligible closed markets.
- A sample of 100 gave each market a 0.0261% chance of inclusion.
- A sample of 1,000 gave each market a 0.2606% chance.
- A sample of 10,000 gave each market a 2.6062% chance.

Lychee selects these samples **without replacement**, so the same market cannot appear twice inside one sample. Because a selected row is removed from the remaining pool, the sequential draws are dependent. Equal inclusion probability does not mean that each draw is independent of the draws before it.

This definition matches standard survey-sampling treatments. [Penn State defines simple random sampling without replacement](https://online.stat.psu.edu/stat506/Lesson01.html) as a design in which every possible sample of `n` units has the same probability of selection. [Statistics Canada](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/edu/power-pouvoir/ch13/prob/5214899-eng.htm) likewise distinguishes simple random sampling with replacement from the more common without-replacement design.

### Simple random sampling with vs. without replacement

| Method | Can the same row appear more than once? | Probability changes after a draw? | Common use |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| With replacement | Yes | No | Resampling methods and some simulations |
| Without replacement | No | Yes | Selecting distinct records from a finite dataset |

Lychee's current Random Sample feature uses the second approach. If you request 10,000 rows and at least 10,000 eligible rows exist, you receive 10,000 distinct rows.

## Why is random sampling important in data analysis?

Large datasets create a tempting mistake: treating more data as automatically better research.

A complete population is ideal when the goal is to calculate an exact total. But many analytical tasks do not begin with an exact total. They begin with exploration:

- What kinds of records exist?
- Is the schema usable?
- Does a proposed calculation behave correctly?
- What does a typical observation look like?
- Which hypotheses deserve a full analysis?
- Can a researcher manually inspect a manageable subset?

Random sampling makes those questions cheaper to investigate while giving every eligible row a known route into the analysis.

It is particularly useful for:

1. **Exploring a large dataset.** A sample lets researchers inspect the structure and range of the data before running an expensive or time-consuming full-population analysis.
2. **Reducing selection bias.** Choosing the first 1,000 rows, the most popular markets, or the records that look interesting introduces a systematic selection rule. Random selection avoids those particular forms of researcher choice.
3. **Testing an analytical workflow.** Filters, joins, groupings, calculations, and charts can be validated on a smaller subset before scaling up.
4. **Estimating population characteristics.** Sample statistics such as a mean or proportion can estimate the corresponding population parameter, provided the sample design and uncertainty are handled correctly.
5. **Creating review or annotation sets.** Researchers can build a manageable collection of records for manual coding, categorization, quality review, or model evaluation.

Random sampling does not eliminate every form of bias. A sample can only represent its **eligible population**. If relevant rows are missing from the dataset, excluded by a filter, or measured incorrectly, random selection cannot repair those upstream problems.

## The main random sampling methods

“Random sampling” is a family of probability-sampling designs, not one universal procedure.

| Sampling method | How it works | When it is useful |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple random sampling** | Selects units directly with equal probability | General exploration and population estimates |
| **Stratified random sampling** | Divides the population into defined groups, then samples within every group | Ensuring important categories or rare groups are represented |
| **Systematic sampling** | Selects units at a regular interval after a random starting point | Ordered frames without problematic periodic patterns |
| **Cluster sampling** | Randomly selects groups, then studies units within the selected groups | Populations naturally organized into geographic or operational clusters |
| **Unequal-probability sampling** | Assigns different inclusion probabilities to different units | Designs where size, rarity, or analytical importance should affect selection |

Lychee's first implementation is **simple random sampling without replacement**. It does not currently perform stratified, cluster, seeded, or weighted sampling.

That distinction becomes important in prediction-market research. If you need every category, year, platform, liquidity tier, or volume band represented, simple random sampling may not be the best final design. [Stratified sampling can reduce estimation error](https://online.stat.psu.edu/stat506/Lesson06.html) when the strata are chosen well, and it prevents rare but important groups from disappearing merely by chance.

For every run, we compared the sample mean with the known population mean:

`Percentage error = ((sample mean - population mean) / population mean) × 100`

No individual mean was within 10% of the population mean, yet the average of the five sample means was $121,586.94—only 0.31% above the population value. The high and low estimates partially offset one another, illustrating why an estimator's repeated-sampling behavior cannot be judged from a single draw.

The right tail still controlled the individual results. Four samples captured at least one market above $10 million; the fifth captured none. Sample 5 contained five such markets and produced the highest mean. None of the five contained a market above $100 million.

That does not mean 1,000 rows are statistically worse than 100. It means that five realized samples are not a monotonic demonstration. Larger samples reduce expected sampling variability, but any particular sample can land closer to or farther from the target than another particular sample.

## Why a larger sample can still look worse

Sample size changes a probability distribution; it does not issue a guarantee about the next draw.

Across every possible simple random sample of size `n`, the sample mean is an unbiased estimator of the population mean. In repeated sampling, its estimates center on the true value. But one realized sample can still miss high or low.

The expected variability normally declines as `n` grows. Yet three features of this experiment keep individual outcomes interesting:

1. **The volume distribution has a long right tail.** A few markets are thousands of times larger than the median market.
2. **The largest markets are rare.** Even a sample containing thousands of rows can miss most of them.
3. **We ran only five samples per size.** Five draws illustrate sampling variability; they do not fully map the sampling distribution.

The correct conclusion is therefore not “1,000 rows performed worse.” It is:

> Larger random samples generally reduce sampling variability, but the path is probabilistic—not a staircase where every new sample must beat every smaller sample.

## Random sample vs. representative sample

The terms are related, but they describe different things.

**A random sample describes the selection process. A representative sample describes how well the selected observations reflect specified characteristics of the population.**

| Random sample | Representative sample |
|---|---|
| Defined by a probability-based selection process | Evaluated by resemblance to the population on relevant characteristics |
| Can have known, equal inclusion probabilities | Can still differ depending on which characteristics are checked |
| Can look unbalanced by chance | May require stratification, weighting, or a larger sample |
| Supports design-based estimates and measures of sampling uncertainty | Is not a guarantee of unbiased selection by itself |

Our samples were random because every eligible market had the same inclusion probability. But an individual sample could contain too many or too few high-volume markets to reproduce the population mean closely.

Representativeness is also not one universal property. A sample might closely match the population's volume bands while underrepresenting politics. It could match category shares while missing the largest markets. Researchers must say **representative of what**.

This distinction is why 5,000 biased observations are not automatically better than 50 well-selected observations—and why 50 random observations are not automatically sufficient for every research question.

## Why the median stabilized faster than the mean

The population mean volume was $121,216.48, but the median was $3,196.22.

The mean uses the magnitude of every value. Add one billion-dollar market to a sample, and its contribution flows directly into the average. The median depends on the ordered middle observation. A massive value at the far end of the distribution does not pull the middle upward in the same way.

That difference appeared across our repeated samples:

| Sample size | Lowest sample median | Highest sample median | Population median |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 100 | $1,886.72 | $7,625.65 | $3,196.22 |
| 1,000 | $2,664.26 | $3,631.41 | $3,196.22 |
| 10,000 | $2,822.89 | $3,046.29 | $3,196.22 |

*The sample median settled near the population median faster than the mean because it was less sensitive to the extreme right tail.*

This does not make the median universally superior. The two statistics answer different questions:

- **Mean volume:** How much volume would each market have if total volume were distributed evenly?
- **Median volume:** What volume separates the lower half of markets from the upper half?

When data is highly skewed, reporting both is usually more informative than declaring one “average” without context. The [NIST Engineering Statistics Handbook](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda35b.htm) recommends graphical examination of skewness and heavy tails because extreme observations can materially change how a dataset should be summarized.

## What is the probability of sampling a whale market?

We defined a “whale” as a market with at least $100 million in volume. The population contained 36 such markets out of 383,707.

For simple random sampling without replacement, the probability of selecting at least one whale is:

`P(at least one whale) = 1 - C(N - K, n) / C(N, n)`

where:

- `N` is the population size;
- `K` is the number of whale markets;
- `n` is the sample size.

| Sample size | Probability of ≥1 market above $10M | Probability of ≥1 market above $100M |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 100 | 11.97% | 0.934% |
| 1,000 | 72.11% | 8.97% |
| 10,000 | 99.9998% | 61.35% |

<PublicChart username="misterrpink" slug="polymarket-whale-market-sampling-probability" />

*Larger samples sharply increase the probability of capturing rare high-volume markets, but even 10,000 rows do not guarantee every part of the extreme tail.*

This calculation connects the population structure directly to the sample results. A small sample can be perfectly random while having almost no chance of containing the observations that make the full-population mean so large.

## When prediction-market researchers should use random sampling

Random sampling is valuable when the research task benefits from a manageable, defensible subset rather than an exact census.

### Explore an unfamiliar archive

Use a random sample to inspect market questions, categories, outcomes, timestamps, identifiers, or metadata without defaulting to the first rows returned by a source.

### Test a research workflow

Before joining hundreds of millions of trades, test filters, calculations, and charts on a smaller market sample. You can then carry validated market identifiers into trade or price-history analysis. For an example of moving from market metadata into time-series research, see our guide to [tracking Polymarket odds over time](/guides/polymarket-odds-over-time).

### Build a manual review set

Researchers studying market wording, resolution quality, category labels, or ambiguous questions can randomly select a manageable set for human inspection.

### Estimate a common population characteristic

A simple random sample can estimate means, proportions, or distributions when the sample size and population variability are appropriate for the precision required.

### Compare repeated samples

Repeated samples reveal how sensitive a conclusion is to the particular observations selected. If the answer changes dramatically across runs, that instability is itself a result worth investigating.

## When simple random sampling is not enough

Random sampling is a tool, not a ritual. Sometimes the full population or a different design better matches the question.

| Research goal | Better approach |
|---|---|
| Calculate exact total historical volume | Use the full eligible population |
| Study the largest or most liquid markets | Filter the tail directly instead of hoping to sample it |
| Compare every market category | Stratify by category, then sample within categories |
| Analyze rare market types | Oversample the rare group and use appropriate weights for population estimates |
| Reproduce an identical set later | Use seeded sampling when available, or save the sampled sheet and identifiers |
| Study change over time | Stratify by period or sample within time windows |
| Compare Kalshi and Polymarket | Define comparable frames on both platforms before sampling |

For this volume experiment, a stratified design could divide markets into the seven volume bands and sample within each band. That would guarantee exposure to the tail and make band-level comparisons easier. But it would answer a different question from our simple random sample, and raw sample averages would require weighting if the sampling fractions differed across bands.

## Limitations of this Polymarket sampling experiment

This analysis is intentionally small enough to understand and reproduce. That also creates limitations.

1. **We ran five samples at each size.** More repetitions would show the sampling distributions more completely.
2. **The samples were unseeded.** We saved each result, but another fresh pull will not return the same markets.
3. **We studied one primary variable.** Volume behaves differently from categories, resolution outcomes, prices, or market duration.
4. **The population is unusually skewed.** These results should not be treated as a universal sample-size rule for every dataset.
5. **The archive has a defined extraction date and scope.** Results can change as the underlying archive is updated or the eligible-population filters change.
6. **A market is not a trade.** Sampling market rows equally is different from sampling trades, traders, dollars, or time periods equally.
7. **We know the population values here.** In ordinary sample-based research, the true population mean and median may not be available for comparison.

The experiment demonstrates why researchers should report the population definition, sampling design, sample size, measured statistic, and uncertainty—not just the word “random.”

## Frequently asked questions about random sampling

### What is simple random sampling?

Simple random sampling is a probability-sampling method in which every unit in the eligible population has the same chance of selection and every possible fixed-size sample has the same probability of being chosen.

### What is a simple random sampling example?

If a dataset contains 100,000 eligible prediction markets and a researcher randomly selects 1,000 distinct markets with equal inclusion probabilities, the resulting subset is a simple random sample without replacement.

### Is random sampling the same as simple random sampling?

Not always. Simple random sampling is one type of random sampling. Stratified, cluster, systematic, and unequal-probability designs also use randomness but structure the selection process differently.

### What is the difference between a random sample and a representative sample?

A random sample is defined by how observations are selected. A representative sample is assessed by how closely the selected observations resemble the population on characteristics relevant to the research question. A random sample can be unrepresentative by chance.

### What does sampling without replacement mean?

Sampling without replacement means a selected observation cannot be chosen again within the same sample. Every row in a Lychee random sample is therefore distinct.

### Does random sampling eliminate bias?

Random sampling reduces selection bias caused by manually choosing observations, but it cannot correct missing coverage, inaccurate measurements, inappropriate filters, nonresponse, or a badly defined target population.

### Does a larger sample always give a more accurate result?

Larger samples generally reduce expected sampling variability, but one individual larger sample is not guaranteed to be closer to the population value than every smaller sample. Population variability and rare extreme observations also matter.

### How large should a random sample be?

There is no universal sample size. The answer depends on the statistic being estimated, population variability, required precision, sampling design, subgroup analysis, and available resources. Rare or heavy-tailed outcomes can require much larger samples than stable, common characteristics.

### Why did the sample median outperform the mean here?

Polymarket volume had a long right tail. A few enormous markets strongly changed the sample mean when included, while the median depended on the middle-ranked market and was less sensitive to those extreme values.

### Can I generate a random sample without Python or Excel?

Yes. Lychee can select a random sample directly from supported historical datasets after filters and other eligible-population operations are applied. The result can be analyzed, charted, saved, or exported in the same workspace.

## The real lesson from random sampling

Our first assumption was that the experiment would mainly demonstrate a new way to select rows.

Instead, random sampling exposed the structure of the underlying market.

Polymarket volume was so concentrated that a tiny number of markets could transform the average of an otherwise valid sample. Increasing sample size made estimates more stable overall, but the process remained probabilistic. The median settled faster because it measured the center without giving the tail the same leverage over the result.

That leads to a better research habit:

> Do not ask only whether a sample was random. Ask what population it came from, which statistic you are estimating, how variable the population is, and how much uncertainty the sample leaves behind.
