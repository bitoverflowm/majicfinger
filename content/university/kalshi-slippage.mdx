# Kalshi Shows 40¢. Why Did Your Order Average 42.8¢?

**Lychee University · Lesson 7**

You find YES offered at 40¢. You want 500 contracts. That should cost $200 before fees.

Except only 100 contracts are available at 40¢.

<QuoteHasQuantityAnimation />

**Slippage on Kalshi means your execution price differs from the price you expected.** One reason is order book depth: there may be too few contracts at the best price to fill your whole order. Buying the rest at higher prices raises your average cost.

The price has a quantity attached. That's the detail worth checking.

## Why can a larger order cost more?

Imagine this hypothetical order book. These are offers to sell YES, not prices taken from a live market:

| YES ask price | Contracts available | Cost to buy that level |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 40¢ | 100 | $40 |
| 42¢ | 200 | $84 |
| 45¢ | 200 | $90 |
| **Total** | **500** | **$214** |

If you buy all 500 immediately and these offers remain available, you spend **$214 before fees**. Divide by 500 and your average execution price is **42.8¢**.

<WalkTheBookAnimation />

That is 2.8¢ per contract above the best ask you first saw: **$14 more** than filling everything at 40¢. The order has *walked the book*, consuming offers at successive prices.

Drag the size below on the same hypothetical book. Stay at or under 100 contracts and the average stays 40¢. Push past that and the average rises.

<SizeSlippageExplorer />

Kalshi documents this behavior for Quick Orders: a larger purchase can span several price levels, and the order panel accounts for the average price. [Read Kalshi's Quick Orders explanation.](https://help.kalshi.com/en/articles/13823810-quick-orders)

## Was that an unexpected price change?

It depends on what you compared.

Against the initial **40¢ best ask**, our order averaged 2.8¢ more. Against an order preview already showing **42.8¢**, an actual 42.8¢ fill has no additional price difference.

<ReferencePriceExplorer />

The first comparison measures the cost of reaching deeper offers. The second checks whether execution changed from the preview. Prices and available quantities can also change before an order fills.

Write down your reference price when measuring slippage. Otherwise, two people can report different numbers for the same trade.

## Is slippage the same as the spread or a fee?

They measure different things.

The **bid–ask spread** is the gap between the highest current buy offer and the lowest current sell offer. **Depth** is the quantity available at each price. **Trading fees** are separate charges.

<SpreadDepthFeesAnimation />

Suppose YES has a 39¢ bid and a 40¢ ask. That 1¢ spread tells you little about whether 500 contracts are available at 40¢. Our example has the same best ask, but only enough there for 100 contracts.

Likewise, heavy past trading doesn't tell you how much is offered right now. [Trading volume measures activity](/guides/what-does-volume-mean-on-kalshi); the current book shows available orders.

## Can a limit order prevent you from paying more?

A buy limit order sets the maximum contract price you will accept. Set it at 40¢ and it cannot fill at 42¢ or 45¢. That controls the price, but does not guarantee you get all 500 contracts. [See Kalshi's limit-order purchase documentation.](https://help.kalshi.com/en/articles/13823813-limit-order-purchase)

<LimitOrderPartialFillAnimation />

In our unchanged example, 100 could fill at 40¢. The rest would need more sellers at your price, or you could cancel the unfilled portion. Fees remain separate from that price limit.

A limit order can also execute immediately when its price matches available offers. “Limit” describes your price constraint; it doesn't automatically mean you're waiting or paying no fees.

## What should you check before placing the order?

Check **your quantity, the available offers, the average price in the preview, and the total including fees**. If you plan to sell before settlement, examine the bids too: buying easily doesn't guarantee an equally easy exit.
