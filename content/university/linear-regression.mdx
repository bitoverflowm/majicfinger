# Linear Regression

**Lychee University · Lesson 4**

[Last lesson](/university/actual-vs-expected-wins), we compared two numbers.

A baseball team’s actual win percentage.

And its [Pythagorean expectation](/university/pythagorean-expectation)—the win percentage estimated from runs scored and runs allowed.

We plotted one on the horizontal axis and the other on the vertical axis.

Each dot was one team’s season.

Now we want to describe the relationship between those numbers.

That’s where linear regression comes in.

## From dots to a line

Look at the scatter plot.

As the scoring-based estimate rises, does actual win percentage tend to rise too?

A straight line can help us describe that pattern.

But where should we put it?

We could draw one by eye.

Someone else might draw it a little higher. Or give it a different slope.

Linear regression gives us a consistent way to fit that line using the data.

With one input variable and one outcome, we call it simple linear regression.

Here, the input is Pythagorean expectation.

The outcome is actual win percentage.

<ByEyeLinesAnimation />

## The regression equation

The fitted line has a simple equation:

$$
\text{Estimated actual win percentage} = \text{intercept} + \text{slope} \times \text{Pythagorean expectation}
$$

The intercept sets the line’s height.

The slope tells us how much the fitted outcome changes as the input increases.

Let’s use a made-up equation:

$$
\text{Estimated actual win percentage} = 0.05 + 0.90 \times \text{Pythagorean expectation}
$$

These numbers are just for illustration. We haven’t fitted them to a real season.

Use decimals for the percentages.

For a team with a Pythagorean expectation of sixty percent:

$$
0.05 + 0.90 \times 0.60 = 0.59
$$

The line gives us an estimated actual win percentage of fifty-nine percent.

<EquationAnatomyAnimation />

The slope, 0.90, means a one-percentage-point increase in Pythagorean expectation corresponds to a 0.9-percentage-point increase along this fitted line.

<RegressionLineExplorer />

And the intercept?

It gives the line’s value when the input is zero.

That helps position the line mathematically. It may have little practical meaning when zero is far outside the data we’re studying.

<InterceptZoomAnimation />

## How do we choose the line?

For each team, compare its actual win percentage with the value on the line.

That vertical difference is called a residual.

$$
\text{Residual} = \text{actual value} - \text{fitted value}
$$

Suppose our example team actually won sixty-two percent of its games.

The line gave us fifty-nine percent.

Its residual is positive three percentage points.

The dot sits above the fitted line.

If it actually won fifty-six percent, its residual would be negative three percentage points.

Below the line.

<ResidualExplorer />

A common fitting method is ordinary least squares.

It squares each residual, adds those squared values together, and chooses the line with the smallest total.

Squaring keeps positive and negative differences from cancelling out.

It also gives larger differences more weight.

<WhySquareAnimation />

That’s what “best fit” means here: the smallest sum of squared vertical differences.

<LeastSquaresExplorer />

## Two lines, two questions

Remember the diagonal from [lesson 3](/university/actual-vs-expected-wins#what-the-gap-tells-us)?

That line marked where actual win percentage equalled Pythagorean expectation.

Sixty percent across. Sixty percent up.

We chose that line as a reference.

The regression line is fitted from the dots.

The equality line asks:

Did this team’s record match its scoring-based estimate?

The regression line asks:

Where does this team sit compared with the relationship fitted across the teams in our data?

<TwoLinesAnimation />

A team can sit above one line and below the other.

They’re different comparisons.

<TwoLinesExplorer />

## What the fitted line tells us

We now have an equation describing how the two numbers relate in our sample.

But the dots still matter.

Some may sit close to the line.

Others may be much farther away.

A fitted value is an estimate. Individual teams can finish above or below it.

<SpreadExplorer />

And fitting a line to one season doesn’t show how accurately it will predict another.

That takes a separate check using data the model hasn’t already seen.

<NewSeasonAnimation />

For now, we’ve taken the next step.

First, a table.

Then, a scatter plot.

Now, a line fitted to the data.

<TableScatterLineAnimation />

We can describe the relationship with numbers—and measure how far each team sits from that line.
