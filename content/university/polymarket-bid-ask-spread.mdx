# Polymarket Shows 65¢. Why Does Buying Cost 67¢?

**Lychee University · Lesson 8**

You open a Polymarket question at 65¢. Buying costs 67¢. Selling pays 63¢.

Three prices. One market. What happened?

<ThreePricesAnimation />

**The bid–ask spread is the gap between the highest price a buyer offers and the lowest price a seller accepts.** The displayed price can sit between them, where nobody is currently offering to trade.

## Which price can you actually trade at?

Imagine this hypothetical YES order book, with enough contracts at each quote for our small example:

| Price | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **63¢ bid** | The best current offer to buy your YES shares |
| **67¢ ask** | The cheapest current offer to sell you YES shares |
| **65¢ midpoint** | Halfway between those quotes |
| **4¢ spread** | The difference between the bid and ask |

<BidAskMidpointAnimation />

To buy immediately against those offers, you pay 67¢. To sell immediately, you accept 63¢. A 65¢ midpoint describes the middle of the quotes; it doesn't create a seller at 65¢.

Polymarket documents that it normally displays the midpoint. When the spread exceeds 10¢, it displays the last traded price instead. Neither display guarantees that your order can execute there. [Polymarket's prices and order book documentation](https://docs.polymarket.com/concepts/prices-orderbook)

## What does the spread cost on a round trip?

Buy 100 YES shares at 67¢: **$67 spent**.

Immediately sell those same 100 shares at 63¢, assuming the quotes and quantities haven't changed: **$63 received**.

You lose **$4 before fees**, about **5.97% of the $67 entry cost**. That's the full 4¢ spread crossed in this buy-then-sell example. Don't count another full spread for each leg.

<RoundTripCostExplorer />

If you instead hold to settlement, there is no sale at 63¢. Your result depends on the contract's payout, your 67¢ purchase price, and applicable fees. The round-trip example measures one particular exit, not every possible trading strategy.

## Does Polymarket collect the spread as a fee?

The spread itself is the gap between traders' quotes. It isn't automatically a platform charge. Trading fees, where applicable, are a separate part of the calculation. [Polymarket's trading-fee explanation](https://help.polymarket.com/en/articles/13364478-trading-fees)

<SpreadVsFeeAnimation />

A market maker may try to buy at 63¢ and sell at 67¢. But both trades must fill to capture that difference. If only the purchase fills and the market falls, the maker is left holding shares that have lost value.

## Can a limit order get you the midpoint?

You can offer to buy at 65¢ instead of accepting 67¢. Someone still has to sell to you. Your order may fill, fill partly, or remain unfilled.

<LimitAtMidpointAnimation />

A limit order specifies your acceptable price. If you submit a buy limit at 67¢ against an available 67¢ ask, it can execute immediately: you are taking an existing offer. Simply choosing a limit order doesn't guarantee you become a maker. [Polymarket's order lifecycle](https://docs.polymarket.com/concepts/order-lifecycle)

## Is a tighter spread always better?

It gives you closer quotes at the top of the book. It doesn't tell you how many shares are available there. A large order may reach worse prices after consuming the first offers.

<TightSpreadDepthExplorer />

For a small trade, compare the bid and ask. For a larger one, also check quantity at each level. Keep fees separate, and distinguish a price someone last paid from a price offered now.
