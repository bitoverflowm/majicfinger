# You Won 90% of Your Bets. Why Are You Still Losing?

**Lychee University · Lesson 11**

You buy 100 contracts across different markets, one contract at a time. Each costs 92¢. Ninety win.

A 90% win rate sounds impressive. Your bill was $92. Your winning contracts pay $90.

<HighWinRateLossAnimation />

**You're down $2 before fees.** Winning often and earning a profit are different measurements. To understand the difference before an outcome is known, calculate expected value.

This example assumes standard binary contracts paying $1 for a winning outcome and $0 for a losing one, all held to settlement. [Polymarket documents binary resolution and redemption.](https://docs.polymarket.com/concepts/resolution)

## What does win rate leave out?

Win rate counts winners relative to the observations being counted. It leaves out the dollars paid and received.

In our hypothetical record:

| Result | Contracts | Profit or loss per contract | Total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Win | 90 | +8¢ | +$7.20 |
| Lose | 10 | −92¢ | −$9.20 |
| **Net** | **100** | | **−$2.00** |

<WinRateCashExplorer />

The winners earned a little each. Each loser cost much more. Different position sizes, early exits, and fees would change the calculation further.

Also check what a tracker calls a “win”: a fill, a closed position, a resolved market, or a whole event. Those counts need not describe the same record.

<WhatCountsAsWinExplorer />

## What is expected value in a prediction market?

Expected value weights each possible profit or loss by its probability. It describes a probability-weighted average, not the result promised on your next trade.

Suppose you estimate a YES contract has a **90% chance of paying $1**, and you can buy it at **92¢**.

Your expected profit per contract, before fees, is:

$$
\mathrm{EV} = 0.90(1 - 0.92) + 0.10(0 - 0.92) = -0.02
$$

That's **−2¢ per contract under your probability estimate**, even though YES is very likely to win. The price asks you to pay more than the $0.90 expected payout.

<ExpectedValueExplorer />

## How do you calculate the break-even price?

For a binary share paying $1 or $0, held to settlement:

$$
\mathrm{Expected\ profit} = p - c - f
$$

Here, **p** is your estimated probability, **c** is the actual purchase price in dollars, and **f** is a known per-contract entry fee in dollars. This simple version assumes no other costs or credits.

With a 90% probability and no fees, the break-even purchase price is 90¢. Paying 88¢ gives **+2¢ expected profit**; paying 92¢ gives **−2¢**. Any applicable entry fee lowers what you can pay while retaining positive expected value.

<BreakEvenPriceExplorer />

That doesn't make an 88¢ purchase automatically good. Your 90% estimate might be wrong, and the quoted 88¢ might not be available for your full order.

## Does positive expected value guarantee a profit?

No. A positive-EV trade can lose, and a negative-EV trade can win.

<PositiveEvCanLoseAnimation />

Even repeated favorable opportunities can produce losses over a limited record. Shared risks and large position sizes also affect how damaging those losses become. A calculation is only as useful as its probabilities, fill assumptions, and cost accounting.

For the broader task of judging a wallet's track record, see [what makes a good Polymarket or Kalshi trader](/university/what-makes-a-good-prediction-market-trader). That lesson examines risk, entry prices, and what happens after you start watching.

## What should you record alongside win rate?

Record the actual cost, quantity, fees, and final sale proceeds or settlement payout. Separate realized results from open positions valued at a stated price and time.

<RecordBesideWinRateAnimation />

Then compare total money earned with total money lost. If you estimate EV before entry, save that estimate too, so later research doesn't quietly replace your original belief with hindsight.

A likely winner can still be expensive. **How often you win matters only alongside what those wins pay—and what the losses cost.**
